Rent and invest in Home equity after selling costs

Home equity = what the home is worth, minus what you still owe the bank, minus selling costs, plus anything the owner invested on months when owning was cheaper than renting. The investing side starts with your deposit and buying costs in the fund, then adds the monthly gap whenever renting is cheaper. Mortgage interest, upkeep and rent are money gone either way. This ignores CPF, tax perks, and the fact that a home is also a place to live. A 25% deposit means a 10% price fall is a 40% hit to your money, and a 10% rise is a 40% gain. Leverage is the whole game with property.

How the numbers work. These numbers use each index's long-run average annual return with dividends reinvested, compounded monthly, before fees, tax and inflation. Real markets don't go up in a straight line; expect years that are down 20–30% on the way. Past returns don't guarantee future ones, and this is education, not personal financial advice. Tickers are examples of index ETFs; Singapore and Hong Kong investors often prefer the Irish-domiciled versions (CSPX, VWRA, IWDA, CNDX) for tax reasons.

Return sources: S&P 500 ≈ 10.6%/yr since 1993 (SPY, dividends reinvested); Nasdaq-100 16.0%/yr vs S&P 500 11.0%/yr, Dec 2007–Dec 2025 (Nasdaq); MSCI World ≈ 12.3%/yr since 2012 (URTH), long-run closer to 8–9%; all-world and emerging-market figures are long-run index averages. Property ≈ 4.5%/yr is a long-run nominal price-growth average for developed markets (US Case-Shiller since 1987 and Singapore's URA private residential index since 1990 both land near 4%); it ignores rental income, leverage and transaction costs, which can swing the real outcome a lot either way.
