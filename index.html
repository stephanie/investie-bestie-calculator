The index funds that beat the pros

Every year S&P runs a scorecard called SPIVA that checks how many professional fund managers beat a plain index. These are the indexes most of them lose to over 15 years. Put 100% in one, or split it across a few. The return shown is the long-run average, not a promise.

How we picked these funds

We started from the categories in S&P's SPIVA scorecards, which compare active fund managers against a plain index, and kept the ones where most managers lose to the index over 10 to 15 years. We then kept one fund for each part of the market, so nothing is listed twice: the biggest US companies (S&P 500), all US companies (Total Market), developed countries (MSCI World), every country (All-World), developing countries (Emerging Markets), smaller companies (Small-Cap), property (REITs) and bonds (Global Bonds). Nasdaq-100 is the exception: it isn't a SPIVA benchmark and is concentrated in tech, but it's included because it's the one people ask about most. Every one is available as a cheap mainstream ETF, including an Irish-domiciled version, which investors outside the US usually prefer because it cuts US tax on dividends and sidesteps US estate tax. The tickers shown are the largest funds tracking each index, not recommendations; any fund tracking the same index will do.

Rent and invest in Home equity after selling costs

Home equity = what the home is worth, minus what you still owe the bank, minus selling costs, plus anything the owner invested on months when owning was cheaper than renting. The investing side starts with your deposit and buying costs in the fund, then adds the monthly gap whenever renting is cheaper. Mortgage interest, upkeep and rent are money gone either way. This ignores local tax breaks, using retirement savings for a deposit, and the fact that a home is also a place to live. A 25% deposit means a 10% price fall is a 40% hit to your money, and a 10% rise is a 40% gain. Leverage is the whole game with property.

How the numbers work. These numbers use each index's long-run average annual return with dividends reinvested, compounded monthly, before fees, tax and inflation. Real markets don't go up in a straight line; expect years that are down 20–30% on the way. Past returns don't guarantee future ones, and this is education, not personal financial advice. Tickers are examples of index ETFs. Investors outside the US often prefer the Irish-domiciled versions (CSPX, VWRA, IWDA, CNDX) for tax reasons.
